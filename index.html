01 Voice Out of the Whirlwind Read analysis

Literary work: The Book of Job

Author: Traditionally associated with an unknown author

Literary lens: Philosophical and Religious Criticism

Background of the Work

The Book of Job is a biblical literary work that explores the problem of human suffering, faith, and divine justice. It tells the story of Job, a righteous man who experiences severe suffering despite his faithfulness. He loses his possessions, children, and health, leading him to question why such misfortune has happened to him.

Literary Analysis

The Book of Job challenges the belief that good people will always experience prosperity and that suffering is necessarily a punishment for wrongdoing. Through Job's experiences, the narrative examines the difficulty of understanding suffering and the limitations of human explanations for life's hardships.

Job's friends attempt to explain his misfortune by suggesting that he must have committed wrongdoing. However, their assumptions fail to acknowledge the complexity of his situation. Their responses demonstrate how people sometimes judge the suffering of others without fully understanding their experiences.

Through a philosophical and religious lens, the work raises important questions about faith, justice, and the relationship between human beings and God. Job's questioning reflects his struggle to reconcile his beliefs with the suffering he experiences. The appearance of God in the whirlwind emphasizes the vastness of creation and the limits of human understanding.

The work remains relevant because people continue to experience hardships that cannot always be explained through simple reasoning. It teaches readers to approach suffering with humility, compassion, and understanding rather than immediately blaming those who experience it.

02 The Panchatantra Read analysis

Author: Traditionally attributed to Vishnu Sharma

Literary lens: Moral and Didactic Criticism

Background of the Work

The Panchatantra is an ancient Indian collection of interrelated animal fables designed to teach practical wisdom and moral lessons. Its stories frequently portray animals that demonstrate human characteristics such as intelligence, greed, loyalty, dishonesty, and pride. Through entertaining narratives, the collection teaches readers about relationships, decision-making, and the consequences of human behavior.

Literary Analysis

The Panchatantra uses animal characters to represent recognizable human behaviors. Although the characters are animals, their actions reflect the decisions and attitudes commonly observed in society. This approach allows readers to understand moral lessons through relatable situations.

Through a moral and didactic lens, the collection emphasizes the importance of wisdom, careful judgment, and awareness of consequences. Some characters succeed because they use intelligence and strategic thinking, while others experience failure because of greed, impulsiveness, or misplaced trust.

The stories also demonstrate that intelligence must be accompanied by responsibility. A person may possess knowledge and cleverness but still make harmful decisions when influenced by selfish interests or excessive confidence.

The Panchatantra remains relevant because people continue to face challenges involving trust, friendship, competition, and decision-making. Its lessons encourage readers to think carefully before acting, evaluate situations critically, and recognize that choices often have consequences beyond their immediate circumstances.

03 The Ramayana Read analysis

Author: Traditionally attributed to Valmiki

Literary lens: Ethical and Cultural Criticism

Background of the Work

The Ramayana is an ancient Indian epic that follows the life of Prince Rama, his exile, the abduction of his wife Sita by Ravana, and the efforts to rescue her. The story explores duty, loyalty, courage, love, and the struggle between opposing forces. It has influenced religious traditions, literature, art, and cultural practices throughout South and Southeast Asia.

Literary Analysis

The Ramayana explores the relationship between personal desires and moral responsibilities. Rama's acceptance of exile demonstrates his commitment to duty, while Lakshmana's decision to accompany him reflects loyalty and devotion. Hanuman's courage and dedication further emphasize the importance of service and cooperation.

Through an ethical and cultural lens, the epic reveals how individual decisions are influenced by family expectations, leadership responsibilities, and societal values. The characters' actions reflect the ideals of the culture in which the narrative developed.

However, the work also allows readers to examine the difficulties that arise when social expectations conflict with personal well-being. Sita's experiences, in particular, invite reflection on gender expectations, dignity, and the treatment of women within traditional societies.

The Ramayana remains significant because its themes continue to encourage discussions about responsibility, loyalty, justice, and personal sacrifice. It reminds readers that fulfilling one's duties can involve difficult decisions and that moral questions should be considered carefully within their cultural and historical contexts.

04 The Mahabharata Read analysis

Author: Traditionally attributed to Vyasa

Literary lens: Psychological and Ethical Criticism

Background of the Work

The Mahabharata is one of the major ancient Indian epics. It centers on the conflict between the Pandavas and the Kauravas, two branches of a royal family whose struggle for power eventually leads to war. The epic explores duty, justice, ambition, family relationships, and the consequences of human decisions.

Literary Analysis

The Mahabharata presents human conflict as a complicated interaction of personal interests, family loyalty, ambition, and moral responsibility. The characters often face situations in which their duties and personal relationships appear to contradict one another.

Through psychological criticism, the epic encourages readers to examine how emotions, desires, and personal experiences influence behavior. Jealousy, pride, resentment, and the desire for power contribute to the growing conflict between the two groups. These emotions demonstrate how personal disagreements can develop into destructive struggles when they are not addressed responsibly.

Arjuna's hesitation before the battle of Kurukshetra illustrates the emotional difficulty of confronting a conflict involving people to whom he is connected. His conversation with Krishna explores questions about duty, action, and moral responsibility.

The epic remains relevant because people continue to experience conflicts between personal feelings and external expectations. It teaches readers to consider the consequences of their choices and recognize how unchecked ambition and resentment can harm individuals, families, and communities.

05 The Iliad Read analysis

Author: Traditionally attributed to Homer

Literary lens: Psychological Criticism

Background of the Work

The Iliad is an ancient Greek epic that focuses on a portion of the Trojan War, particularly the anger of Achilles and its consequences. It explores themes of honor, pride, revenge, grief, courage, and mortality. Rather than narrating the entire war, the poem examines how personal conflicts influence the lives of warriors and the communities surrounding them.

Literary Analysis

The central conflict of The Iliad begins with Achilles' anger following his disagreement with Agamemnon. Feeling dishonored, Achilles withdraws from battle, leaving the Greek army in a vulnerable position. His decision demonstrates how personal pride and the desire for recognition can influence judgment and affect others.

Through psychological criticism, Achilles can be understood as a character whose emotions strongly influence his actions. His anger eventually develops into intense grief after the death of Patroclus, leading him to seek revenge against Hector. This transformation reveals how grief can intensify anger and influence a person's decisions.

The contrast between Achilles and Hector also highlights different dimensions of heroism. While Achilles is known for his extraordinary fighting ability, Hector demonstrates concern for his family and responsibility toward his community. Their experiences reveal that warriors are not simply symbols of strength but human beings who experience fear, love, grief, and loss.

The Iliad remains relevant because pride, anger, and grief continue to influence human relationships and conflicts. The poem reminds readers that the pursuit of honor can become destructive when personal emotions outweigh responsibility and understanding.

06 The Odyssey Read analysis

Author: Traditionally attributed to Homer

Literary lens: Hero's Journey and Psychological Criticism

Background of the Work

The Odyssey is an ancient Greek epic that follows Odysseus as he attempts to return home to Ithaca after the Trojan War. His journey is filled with dangerous encounters, supernatural challenges, temptations, and unexpected obstacles. Meanwhile, his wife Penelope and son Telemachus face their own struggles while waiting for his return.

Literary Analysis

The Odyssey presents the journey home as both a physical adventure and a process of personal development. Odysseus must rely on intelligence, courage, patience, and adaptability to overcome the challenges he encounters.

Through the Hero's Journey lens, his experiences represent a series of trials that test his abilities and character. His encounters with the Cyclops, Circe, and the Sirens demonstrate the importance of strategic thinking and self-control. However, his pride and impulsiveness sometimes worsen his circumstances, showing that even a capable hero has weaknesses.

From a psychological perspective, Odysseus' behavior reveals the influence of curiosity, ambition, determination, and personal attachment. His desire to return to Ithaca reflects his connection to his family, identity, and responsibilities. Penelope's patience and Telemachus' development also demonstrate that endurance and personal growth are not limited to the person undertaking the physical journey.

The Odyssey remains relevant because people continue to face uncertainty, obstacles, and difficult decisions while pursuing their goals. It teaches readers that perseverance is important, but success also requires self-awareness, sound judgment, and the willingness to learn from mistakes.

07 The Song of Roland Read analysis

Author: Anonymous

Literary lens: Historical and Cultural Criticism

Background of the Work

The Song of Roland is a medieval French epic that recounts the heroic death of Roland, a knight serving Charlemagne. The narrative centers on an ambush of Charlemagne's rear guard and explores loyalty, courage, betrayal, religious identity, and the ideals of medieval knighthood.

Literary Analysis

Roland represents the medieval ideals of bravery, loyalty, and honor. His commitment to his ruler and his determination to defend his reputation demonstrate the importance placed on knightly courage. However, his refusal to call for assistance early enough also raises questions about the relationship between bravery and pride.

Through historical and cultural criticism, the poem reflects the values and beliefs of medieval European society. Loyalty to one's ruler, devotion to Christianity, and willingness to sacrifice oneself for a greater cause are presented as important qualities of an ideal knight.

The character of Ganelon illustrates the destructive consequences of betrayal and personal resentment. His actions demonstrate how individual grievances can endanger an entire community. Meanwhile, Roland's decisions reveal that admirable qualities can become problematic when they are pursued without sufficient judgment.

The Song of Roland remains relevant because loyalty, courage, and responsibility continue to be valued in contemporary society. However, the narrative also encourages readers to examine how pride and rigid group loyalties can contribute to conflict. Understanding the poem's historical context allows readers to appreciate its literary significance while critically examining its portrayal of religious and cultural differences.

03 / MY FAVORITE MASTERPIECE

A Story That Stayed With Me

MY FAVORITE WORK

The Odyssey

Epic Poem · Traditionally attributed to Homer

Overview of the Work

The Odyssey follows Odysseus, a Greek warrior who struggles to return to his homeland of Ithaca after the Trojan War. His journey lasts ten years and exposes him to dangerous creatures, supernatural forces, temptations, and the consequences of his own decisions. While Odysseus faces challenges at sea, his wife Penelope and son Telemachus struggle to maintain their household and wait for his return.

Beyond its adventures, the epic explores perseverance, loyalty, identity, pride, and the meaning of home.

Reasons for Choosing the Work

I consider The Odyssey a meaningful literary masterpiece because its central journey can be understood beyond its literal events. Odysseus does not simply need to travel across the sea; he must endure uncertainty, make difficult decisions, and confront the consequences of his actions.

The story demonstrates that personal growth is rarely a straightforward process. People encounter unexpected obstacles, make mistakes, and experience situations that challenge their understanding of themselves. Odysseus' experiences reveal that courage and intelligence are important, but recognizing one's limitations is equally necessary.

Significant Themes and Lessons

One significant theme is perseverance. Despite the obstacles he encounters, Odysseus continues his efforts to return home. However, the epic also demonstrates that perseverance must be accompanied by sound judgment because pride and impulsiveness can create additional difficulties.

Another important theme is loyalty. Penelope's efforts to protect her household while waiting for Odysseus demonstrate patience, intelligence, and determination. Her experiences show that strength can be expressed through quiet endurance rather than physical power.

The theme of home and belonging is also central to the narrative. Ithaca represents more than a physical destination. It symbolizes Odysseus' family, identity, relationships, and responsibilities.

Personal Connection and Realization

Through The Odyssey, I realized that a person's journey is not defined only by reaching a destination but also by the lessons learned along the way. Difficult experiences can reveal both strengths and weaknesses, and recognizing these qualities is an important part of personal growth.

I also learned that courage does not mean making every decision correctly. Sometimes, growth begins when a person acknowledges the consequences of their actions and learns to approach situations differently.

Ultimately, The Odyssey reminds me that achieving a goal is important, but understanding myself, learning from challenges, and appreciating meaningful relationships are also essential parts of the journey.

04 / SUMMARY OF LEARNING

What Great Books Taught Me

Throughout my Great Books course, I learned that literature is more than a collection of stories, poems, and narratives from different periods. Literary masterpieces preserve the values, beliefs, conflicts, and experiences of the societies that produced them. By studying works from different cultural traditions, I began to recognize both the differences between societies and the human concerns they share.

One of my most important learnings was the value of literary lenses. A literary work can be interpreted in different ways depending on the questions a reader asks. Psychological criticism examines the emotions, motivations, and conflicts that influence characters. Ethical criticism explores questions of responsibility, justice, and morality. Historical and cultural criticism examines how a work reflects the beliefs and expectations of the society in which it developed. These approaches taught me that understanding literature requires more than knowing what happens in a story; it also requires examining why its events and themes matter.

The literary masterpieces we studied also demonstrated that human experiences are often complicated. The Book of Job raises questions about suffering and justice. The Panchatantra explores wisdom and the consequences of decisions. The Ramayana and the Mahabharata examine duty, loyalty, and moral responsibility. The Iliad presents the destructive effects of anger and the pain of war, while The Odyssey emphasizes perseverance, identity, and the challenges of returning home. The Song of Roland reflects the ideals of loyalty, honor, and religious identity within a medieval worldview.

Analyzing these works helped me develop my critical thinking and interpretation skills. I learned to look beyond a character's actions, consider different perspectives, identify recurring themes, and connect literary ideas to situations outside the text. I also became more aware that interpretations should be supported by textual evidence rather than personal assumptions alone.

As an AB Psychology student, I find these skills particularly valuable because literature provides opportunities to examine how people respond to suffering, conflict, relationships, social expectations, and difficult choices. Although literary characters cannot represent every real-life experience, their stories can encourage meaningful questions about human behavior.

Overall, Great Books taught me to approach literature with greater curiosity and an open mind. I now see literary analysis as an opportunity to understand not only the text itself but also the cultures, values, and human experiences that give it meaning.

05 / PERSONAL REFLECTION

Looking Back on My Journey

My experience in Great Books helped me understand that reading a literary masterpiece and analyzing it are two different experiences. When reading a story, it is easy to focus on what happens to the characters and how the plot develops. However, analyzing a work requires me to slow down, question the decisions of the characters, and consider the ideas communicated through their experiences. Throughout the course, I learned that the meaning of a story is not always immediately obvious and that understanding it often requires looking beyond its surface.

One challenge I encountered was understanding literary works that came from unfamiliar cultures and historical periods. Their beliefs, traditions, and expectations were sometimes different from those I encounter in everyday life. I realized that interpreting these works using only a present-day perspective could lead to misunderstandings. To understand them better, I needed to consider their historical contexts and examine the values that influenced their characters and events.

Among the literary approaches I encountered, psychological criticism particularly interested me because it allowed me to examine the emotions, motivations, and decisions of literary characters. As an AB Psychology student, I am interested in understanding why people behave the way they do. Literature offers opportunities to explore questions about pride, loyalty, fear, anger, and perseverance. At the same time, I learned that understanding a character does not necessarily mean agreeing with their actions. It means considering their circumstances and motivations while still evaluating the consequences of their decisions.

The literary masterpieces also encouraged me to think more carefully about human nature and society. Different characters experience different struggles, yet many of their experiences involve concerns that remain recognizable today, such as the desire for acceptance, the pressure to fulfill expectations, the consequences of ambition, and the difficulty of making moral decisions. These similarities helped me appreciate how literature can connect people across different periods and cultures.

Moving forward, I can apply the lessons from Great Books to my personal relationships and academic life by becoming more thoughtful when interpreting situations, considering perspectives different from my own, and avoiding conclusions based on limited information. These habits are also valuable in psychology, where understanding people requires careful observation, critical thinking, and sensitivity to context.

Ultimately, my journey through Great Books taught me that literature is not only something to read and analyze for an academic requirement. It can also be a way of questioning assumptions, examining values, and reflecting on the experiences that shape how people understand themselves and others. I may not relate to every character or agree with every lesson presented in a literary work, but each one offers an opportunity to think more deeply. That, for me, is one of the most valuable things I gained from this course.
